  In April 2026, Northeastern went to space for the first time: a student-built CubeSat, deployed from the
  International Space Station as one of a five-satellite constellation. About 15 students built it in under a year,
  working shoulder-to-shoulder with the Pleiades Consortium, a group of five partner university teams led by Cal Poly
  Pomona. Our students worked on both the hardware and the software of the platform, including assembly, integration,
  and testing, with the satellite shipped around the country for environmental testing. We open-sourced all of it.

  Atlas is an educational satellite with a Sony Spresense camera payload for imaging demos. It's part of PROVES, an
  educational satellite program that's also developing university CubeSat kits for under $3,000, lowering the barrier to
  space for students and researchers everywhere. Next up: a mobile ground station to track Atlas and the other PROVES
  satellites.
</Mission>

  TeraLink-1 will fly the first-ever terahertz-frequency space-to-ground link demo. It's a 6U CubeSat funded by AFRL's
  University Nanosatellite Program and built with the Ultrabroadband Nanonetworking Lab (UNLab) at Northeastern's
  Institute for the Wireless Internet of Things, and its payload is a pair of THz-band antennas for 225 GHz uplink and
  downlink.

  After deploying into an ISS orbit and detumbling, TeraLink-1 keeps its solar panels pointed at the sun. On passes over
  campus it relays telemetry and location over UHF while its THz antenna face tracks our [THz ground
  station](#sub-thz). The experiment data is too big for UHF, so it comes down over S-band through three Amazon Web
  Services ground stations.

  The satellite design is complete. After making it through the first round last year, the team is preparing for UNP's
  Critical Design Review in November (eight hours of AFRL judges critiquing our system design and analyses), and
  building a FlatSat for flight-software development (F´ and embedded) and hardware- and software-in-the-loop testing.
  See the [TeraLink-1 org chart](/people#teralink-1) for who's working on it.
</Mission>

  The ground half of TeraLink-1's terahertz link, and a truly unprecedented challenge: a 2 m Cassegrain reflector dish
  with a surface accuracy of 83 micrometers (0.083 mm), carrying two THz-band antennas for 225 GHz uplink and downlink.

  When TeraLink-1 passes over our UHF ground station, it reports its location and telemetry. We turn that into a
  two-line element set (TLE) to predict where it will be, and the THz station uses a star tracker and rotator to point
  the dish and follow it. The satellite stores the uplink and downlink I/Q data from each experiment, then sends it down
  over S-band.

  This semester the team is working on reflector surface-accuracy analysis and fabrication with vendors and NU
  facilities, thermal and structural analyses, hardware procurement, deployment-site preparation, and the electronics
  and software architecture for pointing and tracking.
</Mission>

<Mission id="leonird" name="LEONIRD CubeSat" status="Rising project" collaborators="With the MIT Satellite Team">
  An MIT-led, open-source, payload-agnostic satellite bus that anyone can plug a payload into: three 1U CubeSats, built on PyCubed and moving
  to F´ and Zephyr. The bus is in development, with a formal review process being built and
  a licensing target of December. NSL is helping choose a 1U payload instrument and supporting payload engineering and
  bus design across firmware, software, and mechanics.
</Mission>
